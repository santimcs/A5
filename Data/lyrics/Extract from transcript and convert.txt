Hey Jude
“Hey Jude” by The Beatles is a song of encouragement and comfort, telling someone to stay hopeful and turn pain into something better. It was originally written by Paul McCartney to console John Lennon’s young son Julian during his parents’ divorce, but the lyrics were shaped so they could speak to anyone going through a hard time.

The song urges Jude not to be afraid of love or change, to “take a sad song and make it better” by opening his heart instead of shutting down. It emphasizes resilience and self-belief, suggesting that the strength to move forward and heal is already “on your shoulder,” meaning within yourself. Over time, its long sing‑along ending and gentle advice have turned it into a universal anthem about overcoming sadness, embracing vulnerability, and finding hope in difficult situations.

One Night in Bangkok
“One Night in Bangkok” is about a cynical American chess grandmaster in Bangkok who contrasts his serious focus on a world chess tournament with the city’s famous nightlife and temptations. He looks down on tourists who come for bars, sex, and spectacle, insisting he “gets his kicks” from the intellectual challenge of chess instead of the pleasures around him.[1][2][3]

The song mixes descriptions of real Bangkok locations and red‑light culture with wordplay about chess, showing a clash between discipline (the “ultimate test of cerebral fitness”) and hedonism. At the same time, the lyrics reflect a Western outsider’s dismissive, stereotyped view of the city, which is why the song has been criticized and was even banned in Thailand in the past for creating a misleading, disrespectful image of Thai culture.

A change is gonna come
“A Change Is Gonna Come” by Sam Cooke is a soulful anthem about the pain of racism and injustice, combined with a deep, almost stubborn hope that things will eventually get better. Each verse describes hardship, discrimination, and feeling worn down, while the chorus keeps returning to the belief that a long‑awaited change in society is coming.

Cooke wrote the song after facing segregation and being turned away from a whites‑only motel, and it was also inspired by civil rights events and protest songs like Bob Dylan’s “Blowin’ in the Wind.” Because it so powerfully captures both suffering and hope, it quickly became one of the key musical symbols of the US Civil Rights Movement and is still regarded as one of soul music’s greatest, most important songs.
